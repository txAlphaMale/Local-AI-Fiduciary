# ACA Subsidies, IRMAA & Social Security — Income-Threshold Mechanics Reference (2026)

_Corpus doc for the Fiduciary knowledge RAG (G1/SS companions). Concepts behind `aca.py`/`ss.py` and PLAN → ACA / SOCIAL SECURITY. All three programs gate on income thresholds that interact with Roth conversions and gain harvesting — see also `glossary-tax-retirement` in the in-app WIKI. Not tax or benefits advice._

## ACA premium tax credit (PTC): the subsidy cliff
- Eligibility and subsidy size are driven by **MAGI as a percentage of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL)** for the household size — NOT the same MAGI definition used for NIIT or IRMAA (see the "MAGI means different things" note below).
- Below the PTC floor (100% FPL in states that didn't expand Medicaid, including Texas — this is the **coverage gap**, where income is too low for ACA subsidies but the household doesn't qualify for Medicaid either) there is no subsidy. From the floor up to 400% FPL, the required household contribution toward the benchmark (second-lowest-cost silver) plan rises on a sliding percentage-of-income schedule, and the PTC covers the rest.
- **The 400%-FPL cliff**: at and above 400% FPL, the subsidy schedule historically dropped to ZERO instantly — a single extra dollar of MAGI could cost thousands in lost subsidy. (An enhanced-subsidy provision that capped the required contribution at a flat percentage above 400% FPL, removing the hard cliff, was in effect for several years under separate legislation; verify current-year status via AUDITS → GROUNDING or official Healthcare.gov guidance, since this specific provision's expiration/extension has changed by statute more than once.) The planning implication regardless of which regime is active: know exactly how much MAGI headroom exists before a Roth conversion or a large gain-harvest transaction pushes the household over whatever the effective cliff is that year.
- **ACA MAGI is unusually broad**: it adds back tax-exempt municipal-bond interest AND counts the FULL Social Security benefit (both the taxable and non-taxable portion) — unlike ordinary AGI, and unlike the ACA-specific treatment of capital gains, which counts the ENTIRE realized gain with no LTCG rate preference baked into the MAGI figure itself (the preference shows up in how the gain is taxed, not in whether it counts toward the cliff).

## IRMAA: the Medicare high-income surcharge
- **IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount)** is a surcharge added to standard Medicare Part B and Part D premiums for beneficiaries whose MAGI exceeds tiered thresholds. It is assessed in tiered brackets — the further above the base threshold, the higher the surcharge tier, up to a top tier several times the base premium.
- **The two-year lookback is the critical planning mechanic**: IRMAA for a given year is based on MAGI from TWO YEARS PRIOR (tax year Y−2 determines the Medicare premium in year Y), not current income. This means a single large income event — a Roth conversion, a big capital gain, a home sale — can trigger a surcharge two years later that's easy to forget was ever connected to that decision.
- **Appeals exist for life-changing events**: Form SSA-44 lets a beneficiary request a redetermination using a MORE RECENT year's income if a "life-changing event" (retirement, divorce, death of a spouse, loss of income-producing property, and similar SSA-defined categories) makes the two-year-old MAGI figure no longer representative. A one-time large income event that ISN'T a qualifying life-changing event (e.g., a planned Roth conversion) is NOT appealable this way — it has to be planned around, not appealed after the fact.

## Social Security: PIA, claiming age, and taxation
- **PIA (Primary Insurance Amount)** is computed from **AIME (Average Indexed Monthly Earnings)** — roughly, career earnings indexed for wage growth and averaged over the highest 35 years — through a **progressive bend-point formula**: a high replacement rate applies to the first slice of AIME, a lower rate to a middle slice, and the lowest rate to AIME above the top bend point. This is why Social Security replaces a much higher percentage of income for lower earners than higher earners. PIA is the benefit paid exactly at **Full Retirement Age (FRA)**, which depends on birth year (progressively later for those born after 1937, settling at 67 for those born 1960 or later).
- **Claiming before FRA** permanently reduces the benefit (an early-reduction schedule, steeper for the first 36 months early than for further months); **claiming after FRA up to age 70** adds **delayed retirement credits**, permanently increasing the benefit. There is no benefit to delaying past 70. This claiming-age decision is one of the largest single levers in a retirement plan and interacts with survivor benefits, tax-bracket management, and the ACA subsidy cliff — it should be modeled as part of the whole plan, not decided in isolation.
- **Taxation of benefits (IRC §86, the "provisional income" worksheet)**: up to 85% of Social Security benefits can be federally taxable, determined by comparing "provisional income" (other income + tax-exempt interest + half of the SS benefit) against statutory thresholds. Those thresholds are NOT inflation-indexed, meaning a steadily larger share of benefits becomes taxable over time for most retirees even with no change in real income — a slow, largely invisible tax increase baked into the law's design.
- **The earnings test** (only relevant before FRA while still working): benefits are withheld above an earnings threshold, at a defined withholding ratio, but this is a TIMING effect, not a permanent loss — withheld amounts are recouped later via a benefit recalculation once FRA is reached.
